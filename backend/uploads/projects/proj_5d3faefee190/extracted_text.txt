Socio-Economic Stratification and
Income Dynamics in Singapore: A
Comprehensive Analysis of 2022
Household Financial Trends
The fiscal year 2022 serves as a pivotal case study in the resilience and structural complexity of
Singapore’s economy. As the city-state emerged from the profound disruptions of the global
pandemic, it was immediately confronted with a dual-pressure environment: a robust recovery
in domestic labor demand and a sharp, exogenous inflationary surge that reached levels not
seen in decades.1 The Singapore Department of Statistics (SingStat), in its annual "Key
Household Income Trends" report, unveiled a landscape where nominal wage growth was
significant, yet the lived experience of many residents was tempered by the eroding effects of
rising consumer prices.1 To understand the socio-economic reality of 2022, one must look
beyond headline figures to the granular shifts in income deciles, the progressive nature of
state-led interventions, and the underlying demographic transitions that continue to reshape
the definition of the Singaporean household.1
Macroeconomic Context: The Tension Between
Growth and Inflation
The economic narrative of 2022 was dominated by the recovery of the labor market.
Employment reached 4.13 million by the middle of the following years, but the seeds of that
growth were sown in the 2022 post-lockdown reopening.5 For resident employed
households—those with at least one working person and a Singapore citizen or permanent
resident as the reference person—this translated into a 6.1 per cent nominal increase in median
monthly household income from work.1 In absolute terms, this figure rose from $9,520 in 2021
to $10,099 in 2022.1
However, this nominal gain occurred alongside a 6.1 per cent increase in the Consumer Price
Index (CPI) for all items, the highest inflationary peak in recent history.1 When adjusted for this
inflation, the real growth in median household income was a marginal 0.2 per cent.1 This
suggests that for the middle-income Singaporean family, 2022 was effectively a year of
financial stasis; the increased dollar amounts in their paychecks were almost entirely absorbed
by the rising costs of utilities, food, and transport.1 This stagnation in real household-level
income is a critical metric, as it highlights the challenge of maintaining purchasing power in an
open economy sensitive to global supply chain shocks.1
The longitudinal view provides even more sobering context. From 2017 to 2022, the real median

monthly household income from work grew by a cumulative 2.9 per cent, which averages to a
modest 0.6 per cent per annum.1 This represents a significant deceleration from the 2012–2017
period, where real growth was 15.5 per cent cumulatively, or 2.9 per cent per annum.2 This shift
reflects a maturing economy where rapid productivity gains are harder to achieve and
suggests that the 2022 "flatness" was not merely a one-off event but part of a broader slowing
trend in real income appreciation.2
Demographic Levers: The Impact of Shrinking
Household Sizes
While household-level income remained flat in real terms, the per-member statistics tell a
different story. In Singapore’s statistical reporting, "household income per household member"
is derived by dividing the total household income from work by the number of members in the
household.1 This metric is often considered a more accurate reflection of individual well-being
as it accounts for the resources available to each person within the domestic unit.2
In 2022, the median monthly household income from work per member rose from $3,027 in
2021 to $3,287, a nominal increase of 8.6 per cent.1 Even after adjusting for inflation, the real
growth per member was 2.6 per cent—significantly higher than the 0.2 per cent growth at the
household level.1 The primary driver for this discrepancy was the continued contraction of the
average household size. Among resident employed households, the average size fell from 3.32
persons in 2021 to 3.27 in 2022.2
This demographic shift functions as a statistical lever. As families become smaller—due to
declining fertility rates and a trend toward nuclear family units—the same amount of total
income is distributed among fewer people, thereby increasing the per-capita resource pool.2
However, critics argue that this "improvement" can be misleading. A smaller household size
may reduce per-member resource strain, but it also reduces the "economies of scale" inherent
in larger families, such as shared utility bills and bulk food purchasing.3 Furthermore, the
reduction in household size is partly attributed to adult children moving out later or couples
choosing not to have children, which has long-term implications for the dependency ratio and
the sustainability of the social security system.4

Distributional Analysis: Decile-Specific Outcomes
The aggregate median often masks the divergent experiences of different socio-economic
tiers. SingStat divides the population into ten equal deciles based on household income per
member.1 In 2022, the distribution of income growth was notably progressive, meaning that
those at the lower end of the spectrum generally saw higher percentage increases in their real
income compared to those at the top.1
The Bottom of the Distribution: Low-Wage Recovery
The first decile (the bottom 10%) recorded a remarkable real income growth per member of
10.1 per cent in 2022.1 This was the highest growth rate among all groups and can be attributed
to several structural factors. First, the 2022 labor market reopening created high demand in
sectors like food services, retail, and cleaning, which are traditionally staffed by lower-wage
workers.1 This "tight" labor market gave workers in these sectors more leverage to command
higher wages.1
Second, the Singapore government’s labor interventions, particularly the Progressive Wage
Model (PWM), began to yield significant dividends. The PWM, which mandates a minimum
wage ladder and career progression for specific industries, was expanded in 2022 to cover
more workers in sectors like retail and waste management.9 This mandated wage floor
effectively pulled up the earnings of the bottom decile at a rate that far exceeded inflation.9
The Middle-Class Experience
Households in the 2nd to 9th deciles saw real income growth ranging from 1.1 per cent to 5.3

per cent.1 While these gains were positive, they were less dramatic than those at the very
bottom. For middle-income households, the primary income driver was the 6.5 per cent
nominal increase in total wages reported for the private sector in 2022.12 However, because this
group does not always benefit as directly from low-wage interventions like the PWM, their real
wage growth (after 6.1% CPI) was closer to the 0.4 per cent mark reported by the Ministry of
Manpower (MOM) for general wage changes.12
The Top Decile and the Wealth Effect
In a rare reversal of historical trends, the top 10 per cent of households recorded a decline in
real household income per member of 1.3 per cent in 2022.1 This does not necessarily imply
that the wealthy became "poorer," but rather that specific statistical and inflationary factors
converged on this group. One primary reason was the change in household size for this decile;
while other groups saw shrinking families, the top decile's household size actually increased
from 2.26 persons in 2021 to 2.34 in 2022.1 This increase in the denominator of the per-member
calculation naturally pulled the average down.1
Additionally, the top earners were more exposed to the specific inflationary drivers of 2022.
The price of Certificate of Entitlement (COE) for private vehicles and the cost of discretionary
services rose significantly faster than the general CPI.1 Since higher-income households spend
a larger proportion of their income on these luxury or high-end items, their "effective" inflation
was higher than that of lower-income groups during certain periods of the year.1

The Mandated Savings Component: Analyzing the CPF
Impact
A crucial nuance in Singapore's income data is the inclusion of employer Central Provident
Fund (CPF) contributions. The CPF is a compulsory social security system where both
employers and employees contribute a portion of the monthly salary.1 For workers aged 55 and
below, the standard employer contribution is 17 per cent, and the employee contribution is 20
per cent.7
When SingStat reports a median household income of $10,099, it includes the 17 per cent
contributed by the employer.1 While this is technically "income from work," it is not liquid
disposable income available for immediate spending. Critics of this methodology, such as
independent financial analysts, point out that this "pads" the numbers to look more impressive
than the actual take-home pay might suggest.7
For example, if a median individual salary is quoted at $5,000 including employer CPF, the
breakdown is as follows:
●​ Total Salary Package: $5,000 (117% of basic wage).
●​ Basic Wage: ~$4,274.

●​ Employee CPF Contribution (20% of basic): ~$855.
●​ Employer CPF Contribution (17% of basic): ~$726.
●​ Take-Home Pay: ~$3,419.
In this scenario, while the statistical "income" is $5,000, the household only sees approximately
$3,400 in their bank account for discretionary spending.7 This distinction is vital for
understanding why many Singaporeans feel "asset rich but cash poor." The majority of the
$10,099 median household income is locked in CPF accounts, primarily intended for housing
mortgage payments, healthcare (MediSave), and long-term retirement.7
Inequality and the Redistributive Effect of the State
The Gini coefficient is the standard international metric for measuring income inequality, where
0 represents perfect equality and 1 represents total inequality.1 Singapore’s 2022 data shows a
dual-track reduction in inequality.
Market Gini vs. Adjusted Gini
The Gini coefficient based on household income from work per member—before any
government intervention—fell from 0.444 in 2021 to 0.437 in 2022.1 This "market-based" decline
reflects the aforementioned strong wage growth at the bottom (10.1% real) compared to the
decline at the top (-1.3% real).1
However, the most significant impact on inequality comes from the state’s fiscal policy. After
adjusting for government transfers and taxes, the Gini coefficient dropped further to 0.378.1
This was the second-lowest adjusted Gini coefficient on record for Singapore, superseded only
by the 2020 record of 0.375.1 The reduction of 0.059 points (from 0.437 to 0.378) illustrates the
extent to which the Singapore government uses a "transfer and tax" model to moderate the
disparities generated by its high-performance market economy.1
The Scale of Government Support in 2022
To cushion the impact of the 2022 GST hike and high inflation, the government rolled out an
enhanced suite of support measures, including the Assurance Package and the Cost-of-Living
Special Payment.1 Resident households received an average of $5,765 per household member
in government transfers during 2022, a 9.7 per cent increase from the $5,257 received in 2021.1
These transfers are highly progressive and tied to the annual value of the household’s dwelling.
Those living in smaller, 1- and 2-room HDB flats received an average of $12,189 per member,
more than double the national average.1 In contrast, those in 3-room HDB flats received
roughly half that amount (approx. $6,094), and those in private property received even less.1
This "dwelling-type" proxy for wealth allows the government to target fiscal aid with a high
degree of precision without requiring a complex means-testing of every individual resident.14

The Housing Hierarchy: Income and Residence
The correlation between dwelling type and household income remained robust in 2022.
Housing in Singapore is broadly categorized into public (HDB) and private
(Condominiums/Landed).16 Census 2020 data, which provides the most recent granular
geographic and structural breakdown, shows that 4-room HDB flats are the most common
dwelling type, and their income levels often mirror the national median.16
In 2022, the average household income from work for a 4-room HDB household was
approximately $11,097, which aligns closely with the gross median monthly household income.16
In contrast, households in private properties (condominiums or landed estates) typically earned
well above $20,000 per month.16 For example, 44.5 per cent of condominium residents have a
household income exceeding $20,000, and only a negligible 0.17 per cent of 1- and 2-room
HDB dwellers reach that level.18

Geographic Income Disparities
The planning areas of residence also reflect these income stratifications. According to
Census-derived data, areas with high concentrations of private housing and 5-room flats, such
as Bukit Timah and Bishan, show a significantly higher proportion of households in the top
income brackets.17
In Bukit Timah, a significant number of households—over 11,000 out of a total 24,612—earn
more than $20,000 a month.17 In contrast, Ang Mo Kio, a more traditional HDB heartland with a
total of 60,220 households, only has about 6,466 households in the $20,000+ bracket.17 The
middle-income "bulge" (those earning $5,000 to $10,000) is much more pronounced in areas
like Bedok and Choa Chu Kang, highlighting where the "broad middle" of the 2022 income
trends resides.17
Labor Market Dynamics: Sector and Occupation
The 2022 recovery was not uniform across industries. As the economy reopened, sectors like
Financial and Insurance services and Information and Communications saw strong growth, with
median salaries in these fields reaching $8,190 and $7,000 respectively.8 In contrast,
Accommodation and Food Services, while recovering, remained at the bottom of the wage
scale with a median of $2,640.8
The PMET vs. Non-PMET Gap
The distinction between PMETs (Professionals, Managers, Executives, and Technicians) and
non-PMETs is a central feature of the Singaporean workforce. PMETs consistently earn more
than double the wages of non-PMET roles.8 In 2024 (projecting from 2022 trends), the median
income for a PMET was $7,308, while non-PMET roles like cleaners, laborers, and sales workers
saw medians around $2,000 to $3,000.8
Occupational medians in 2022 were roughly:
●​ Managers & Administrators: $10,820.8
●​ Professionals: $8,363.8
●​ Associate Professionals: $4,735.8
●​ Cleaners & Laborers: $2,070.8
This occupational gap is where much of the income inequality measured by the Gini coefficient
originates. Even with the Progressive Wage Model boosting the bottom end, the "ceiling" for
manual and service labor remains significantly lower than the "floor" for administrative and
professional roles.9
Age-Income Trajectory
In Singapore, income typically peaks during the 40–49 age bracket, reaching a median of

approximately $7,498.8 This reflects the "seniority premium" where workers achieve high-value
roles and senior positions after roughly 15 to 20 years in the workforce.19 After age 50, median
wages begin to decline, dropping to $4,731 for those in their late 50s and $3,052 for those 60
and above.8 This "U-shaped" curve of earnings presents a challenge for the aging population, as
many residents find their earning power diminishing just as their healthcare needs and
dependency burdens (elderly parents or adult children) may be increasing.6
Living Standards and the Adequacy of $10,000
While a median household income of $10,099 sounds robust by international standards, the
high cost of living in Singapore necessitates a closer look at "Minimum Income Standards"
(MIS).9 The MIS research, led by Dr. Ng Kok Hoe and other academics, seeks to determine what
a household actually needs to afford more than just survival—to participate in society and have
a "sense of belonging".11
The MIS Benchmarks for 2022
The 2022 MIS budgets indicated that a couple with two children (aged 7–12 and 13–18) required
$6,693 per month to meet basic needs.11 For a single parent with one young child, the budget
was $3,369.11
When comparing these needs to actual 2022 incomes, a gap appears at the bottom. Even
though the national median individual wage of $5,070 was 1.7 times the required per-parent
"living wage" of $2,990, approximately 30 per cent of working households still earned less than
the amount required to meet the MIS standard.9 This group, often referred to as the "working
poor," consists largely of those in the 1st to 3rd deciles.9 For these families, the "average"
success of the 2022 economy feels distant; they remain in a state of financial precariousness
where inflation is a daily threat to their basic security.9
Conclusion: Synthesis of the 2022 Household
Landscape
The research into Singapore’s 2022 monthly household income reveals a city-state navigating a
transition between high-growth history and a high-cost future. The year was defined by a
statistical "stagnation" at the median household level (0.2% real growth), which was masked by
a "demographic improvement" at the per-member level (2.6% real growth).1 The government’s
targeted interventions succeeded in narrowing the income gap, as evidenced by the falling Gini
coefficient and the double-digit real growth for the lowest-earning decile.1
However, the structural reliance on non-employment income (CPF interest) for the poor and
the heavy "padding" of income figures with employer CPF contributions suggest that the
financial resilience of the average household is more fragile than the $10,099 headline figure
suggests.7 Furthermore, the clear stratification by dwelling type and the persistent "MIS gap"

for 30 per cent of the population indicate that social mobility remains a challenge.9 As the
population ages and household sizes continue to shrink, the mechanisms of 2022—progressive
transfers and mandated labor wage floors—will remain the primary tools for maintaining social
cohesion in an increasingly expensive global hub.1

# DBS CEO pay rises to S$15.38 million after bank makes record S$8.19 billion net profit

> *Remuneration includes salary, cash bonus, and non-cash components.*

*Winnie Li | March 09, 2023, 01:31 PM*

Piyush Gupta, the chief executive officer (CEO) of DBS Group, received a remuneration of S$15.38 million in 2022, which comprises salary, cash bonus, and non-cash components, according to the bank's 2022 annual report released on Mar. 9.

This translates to a 13.3 per cent increase from his remuneration in 2021, which amounted to S$13.57 million, according to the group's 2021 annual report.

The increase in Gupta's remuneration was made public together with the bank's announcement that it achieved a record S$8.19 billion of net profit in 2022, marking a 20 per cent increase from 2021.

Gupta has been the CEO and director of DBS Group since 2009.

## Remuneration a recognition of Gupta's performance: DBS

According to the 2022 annual report, Gupta's remuneration was a recognition of his contribution to the bank's stellar performance in 2022, despite it being "a challenging year marked by high inflation, glow growth, and bearish markets".

In addition to scoring a record net profit and cementing its franchise throughout Asia, DBS was named by Global Finance as the "World's Best Bank" and ranked 29th globally in the "Top 100 Best Workplaces for Innovators" list.

It is the only Singapore-headquartered company to be featured on the list.

The bank has also continued making progress on the sustainability front, according to its 2022 sustainability report.

In 2022, DBS established a new chapter under the DBS Foundation, which committed S$5.6 million to enhance financial and digital literacy, as well as reduce food waste, in Asia.

By the end of the year, the bank also reportedly achieves carbon neutrality in its own operations.

The institutional context in March 2023 was shaped not only by financial data but by the
strategic philosophy of the bank’s board and the broader public sentiment regarding income
disparity in Singapore.
DBS Board of Directors: Pay-for-Performance Philosophy
The DBS Board, led by Chairman Peter Seah, remained committed to a "pay-for-performance"
culture, which they believe is essential for driving long-term shareholder value and sustainable
business growth.9 This philosophy is operationalized through a "balanced scorecard" that
assesses the bank's performance across four key areas:
1.​ Shareholder Performance: Evaluated through metrics such as ROE, net profit, and total
shareholder return.10
2.​ Customer Indicators: Measuring the quality of service, digital engagement, and
franchise strength.10
3.​ Employee Indicators: Focusing on engagement levels, diversity, and talent
development.10
4.​ Strategic Focus Areas: Including progress in digital transformation, risk management,
and the sustainability agenda.9
In the 2022 Annual Report, the board stated that the CEO's remuneration was a direct
reflection of the bank’s "banner year" and its delivery against these scorecard goals.1 The board
also emphasized the importance of benchmarking total compensation against peer
organizations of similar size and standing to attract and retain global talent.9
Public Discourse on Income Inequality and Cost of Living
The release of the record-breaking banking profits and CEO remuneration packages in March
2023 coincided with a period of heightened public discourse regarding income inequality in
Singapore.38 Major local news outlets, including The Straits Times, CNA, and Mothership,
reported extensively on the intersection of these topics.

"sandwich class"—middle-income earners who might not qualify for the full suite of
government subsidies but were heavily impacted by rising rental and healthcare costs.36

Summary of Institutional Stability and External Friction
As of March 2023, DBS Group Holdings presented a profile of exceptional institutional strength,
underpinned by record profits, a resilient capital base, and a clear leadership strategy.1 The
institution’s "pay-for-performance" model was functioning as intended, providing significant
rewards for record-breaking ROE and profit.1
Simultaneously, the bank operated within a socio-economic context where the broader
Singaporean population was grappling with
core inflation, a major tax transition, and
rising concerns about the equitable distribution of wealth.24 The technical dossier of March
2023 thus reflects a dual reality: an institution at its financial peak and a domestic environment
characterized by significant cost-of-living adjustments and evolving public expectations

regarding the social compact.1
