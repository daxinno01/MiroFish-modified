Historical Foundations and Strategic Expansion
The establishment of the Development Bank of Singapore Limited (DBS) on 16 July 1968
marked a foundational milestone in the economic sovereignty of Singapore.1 Born out of a
United Nations industrial survey mission led by Dr. Albert Winsemius, the institution was
conceptualized by then-Finance Minister Dr. Goh Keng Swee to take over the industrial
financing responsibilities of the Economic Development Board (EDB).1 Operating initially with a
startup capital of S$100 million, the bank’s primary mandate was to finance Singapore's rapid
industrialisation and urban development projects, providing long-term loans, equity stakes, and
financial guarantees to nascent industries like Jurong Shipyard and National Iron and Steel
Mills.1 This state-backed development model mobilized domestic savings to catalyze high-tech,
value-added sectors, effectively transitioning Singapore from an entrepôt trade hub to a modern
manufacturing economy.3
By the 1970s, DBS began its transition from a specialized development lender into a full-service
commercial bank, opening its first retail branch in the Jurong industrial estate in 1972 and
expanding into merchant banking and securities underwriting.1 The bank’s local dominance was
permanently cemented on 16 November 1998 through the landmark acquisition of the
state-owned Post Office Savings Bank (POSB) for S$1.6 billion.1 Originally founded in 1877 by
the British colonial government to foster savings among lower-income citizens, POSB had
evolved into a beloved national institution popularly termed the "People's Bank".6 The
acquisition allowed DBS to absorb over four million customers, securing a dominant domestic
deposit market share and integrating POSB's expansive heartland automated teller machine
(ATM) network.2
To reflect its pan-Asian aspirations and its evolution from a domestic development finance
agency into a global financial services platform, the institution was officially renamed DBS Bank
Ltd on 21 July 2003.1 This rebranding heralded decades of aggressive regional consolidation,
characterized by strategic acquisitions in major Asian growth corridors, including Hong Kong,
India, and Taiwan.4

The modern era of DBS is largely synonymous with the tenure of Piyush Gupta, who served as
CEO for more than fifteen years.8 Under his stewardship, the bank underwent a radical
technological overhaul, transitioning from a regional commercial lender to a globally recognized
digital pioneer.3 By redesigning its technical architecture and fostering a start-up culture, Gupta
led DBS to be named "World's Best Bank" and "World's Best Digital Bank" by prominent
international financial publications.8 His tenure was characterized by high return on equity
(ROE) ratios and geographic diversification, creating a highly profitable regional platform.4
Following a decade-long internal succession planning process, Tan Su Shan was appointed as
Deputy CEO in August 2024 and subsequently assumed the role of CEO on 28 March 2025,
becoming the first woman to lead Singapore's largest financial institution.8 Tan brought more
than 35 years of consumer banking, wealth management, and institutional banking experience
across financial centers including London, Tokyo, and Hong Kong.8 Since joining DBS in 2010,
she built the foundations of its modern Wealth Management franchise before heading both the
Consumer Banking and Institutional Banking divisions, which collectively generate
approximately 90% of the bank's total income.8
Tan's corporate strategy emphasizes continuity with a distinct focus on emerging opportunities.
Rather than dismantling the digital legacy of her predecessor, she has championed the concept
of a "generative AI-enabled bank with a heart," seeking to fuse advanced artificial intelligence
(AI) with human empathy and the bank's historic development roots.10 Her strategic focus
centers on core Asian markets, specifically China, India, and Indonesia, which offer diverse,
non-correlated growth trajectories.10 Under her leadership, the bank continues to deploy capital
and technology to address major structural shifts in Asia, such as aging populations, rapid
urbanization, and climate transition.10 In late 2025, she was featured in Fortune's Most Powerful
Women in Business list, ranking 6th globally, indicating strong institutional confidence in her
strategic leadership.12

Technology Transformation and the Paradox of Digital
Resilience
DBS’s technological journey is characterized by a bold structural transition that has
simultaneously driven immense profitability and highlighted operational vulnerabilities. Seeking
to avoid the legacy constraints of traditional banking, DBS embarked on its "Digital to the Core"
strategy under the internal rallying cry of "GANDALF".3 This acronym represented Google,
Amazon, Netflix, Apple, LinkedIn, and Facebook, with the letter "D" positioned at the center to
symbolize the bank's intent to operate as a technology firm that happens to offer financial
services.10
Partnering with McKinsey, the bank restructured its legacy systems into a modular operating
model organized around 33 distinct platforms.10 Each platform operates under a "two-in-a-box"
leadership framework, where a business leader and a technology leader share joint key
performance indicators (KPIs) to bridge the historical gap between commercial requirements
and software engineering.10 Through its industrialized data platform, known as ALAN, DBS
reduced its end-to-end AI model deployment time from 18 months to less than five months,
deploying over 1,500 models to generate S
1.1
billion by late 2025.10 To mitigate the risks of generative AI, DBS utilizes a "human-in-the-loop"
approach and adheres to the "PURE" framework, ensuring data use is Purposeful, Unsurprising,

Respectful, and Explainable.10
Despite these digital milestones, DBS’s rapid technological scaling exposed significant stability
risks, leading to several high-profile digital banking service outages between 2021 and 2024.17

The repeated disruptions in 2023, which blocked an estimated 2.5 million payment and ATM
transactions during a single October event, prompted swift and severe intervention from the
Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS).17 In May 2023, the regulator directed DBS to apply a
1.8 times multiplier to its risk-weighted assets for operational risk, effectively locking up
approximately S$1.6 billion in regulatory capital.2
This was followed on 1 November 2023 by a strict six-month pause on non-essential IT

modifications, a prohibition on acquiring new business ventures, and a directive freezing any
reductions to the bank's physical branch and ATM footprint.18 This action sought to ensure that
alternative transactional channels remained available to the public in the event of further digital
downtime.18 In response to these disruptions, DBS's board held senior management
accountable, implementing a 30 percent pay cut to CEO Piyush Gupta's 2023 variable
compensation and a collective 21 percent pay reduction for the bank's management
committee.19
Although MAS lifted the six-month IT modification freeze on 30 April 2024 after noting
substantive improvements in DBS’s technology risk governance, incident management, and
system resilience, the regulator chose to retain the 1.8 times risk-weighted asset capital
surcharge.19 A brief, two-hour service disruption on 2 May 2024—just two days after the
restriction was lifted—highlighted the structural complexity of the bank’s systems transition.20
This event emphasized that technology resilience cannot be achieved solely through short-term
remediation, but requires a multi-year effort to simplify system architecture and transition toward
Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) practices.3 To address these issues, DBS committed a special
S$80 million budget in late 2023 and restructured its IT divisions to prioritize systems stability
over rapid product deployment.17

Workplace Culture and Employee Sentiment
The execution of DBS’s digital and regional strategy is supported by a structured,
high-performance corporate culture. To ensure its workforce can support its digital transition,
DBS established structured upskilling programs like "Digify" and the "DBS Academy".10 These
initiatives provide comprehensive training in agile methodologies, big data analytics,
journey-based product design, and AI application.10 This focus on talent development is
reflected in the bank's compensation philosophy, which links performance metrics to digital
value creation and includes employee share ownership opportunities.5

Nuanced Conclusions and Strategic Outlook
As DBS navigates the complex economic landscape of late 2025, several structural themes will
shape its long-term trajectory. The bank’s transition from a domestic development bank to a
digital-first regional leader provides a compelling case study in corporate transformation. This
evolution has delivered strong returns and positioned the bank to navigate structural shifts,
though not without operational challenges.
The integration of POSB since 1998 highlights the value of maintaining a dual physical-digital
strategy.2 While DBS has pursued digital-only expansion in markets like India, POSB’s physical
presence in Singapore’s heartlands serves as an important trust engine and a safeguard against
digital exclusion.6 Under its "Neighbours first, bankers second" motto, POSB provides a visible
point of contact that helps insulate the broader group from reputational damage during digital
disruptions.6 This model suggests that successful retail banking expansion in diverse Asian
markets requires a balanced approach combining digital efficiency with trusted physical
touchpoints.4
Additionally, the contrast between DBS’s digital innovation and its operational stability highlights
a key strategic trade-off. The platform-based operating model and advanced analytics engines
have driven significant fee growth and return on equity.10 However, the service outages between

can introduce new systemic risks.2 To protect its core franchise, DBS must ensure that its focus
on system resilience and site reliability engineering keeps pace with product development,
shifting key performance metrics from pure speed-to-market toward systemic durability.3
Finally, the bank’s significant ESG and transition finance commitments provide a strategic
competitive advantage.25 As some global institutions pull back from sustainability targets due to
domestic political pressures, DBS’s focus on transition finance allows it to capture a leading
share of green infrastructure mandates in emerging Asian markets.29 By actively participating in
carbon taxonomies and coal phase-out frameworks, DBS is positioning itself to lead the
underwriting of Asian transition debt.28 In the institutional banking space, this green
infrastructure focus acts as an important differentiator, helping the bank sustain profitable,
high-value corporate relationships and mitigate net interest margin compression as interest
rates ease.14

=== Humans of DBS Context.md ===
**"Humans of DBS"** is an ongoing storytelling series that highlights the diverse personal and professional journeys of DBS Bank employees.

The narrative style is first-person, deeply personal, and structured to showcase how individual employee experiences shape the bank's customer service, security, and technological innovation.

---

### Core Themes of "Humans of DBS"

* **Frontline Vigilance and Customer Protection:** A significant focus of the series is placed on retail bank tellers and branch managers who act as the final line of defense against financial scams. These stories highlight the emotional manipulation faced by vulnerable or elderly customers, and the empathy, patience, and training required by staff to intervene and secure customer savings.

* **The Intersection of Human Intuition and AI:** The series frequently highlights how employees utilize, evaluate, and monitor advanced technology like Machine Learning (ML), Generative AI, and voice bots to manage large-scale workflows. Whether monitoring a nationwide 1,200-ATM network, running an procurement "AI control tower", assessing automated fraud detection signals, or managing AI customer service chatbots, a recurring message is that technology handles repetitive tasks so humans can focus on empathy, judgment, and complex decision-making.

* **Career Mobility and Upskilling:** Many posts showcase long-term employee retention, featuring staff members who have been with the bank for anywhere from six to over forty years. The stories emphasize internal mobility—such as transitioning from a part-time gap-year customer service officer to a full-time Gen AI Evaluator, or rising from a 20-year-old teller with O-Level credits to a seasoned Branch Service Manager. The bank is depicted as providing the necessary upskilling workshops and on-the-job training to help employees adapt to technical roles from non-traditional backgrounds, such as hospitality.

* **Workplace Inclusivity and Personal Balance:** The series dives into the human elements of corporate life, featuring stories of working mothers who successfully advanced into senior leadership roles after receiving structural career support during pregnancy. It also highlights operational staff navigating the physical toll of 12-hour rotational shift work, sharing the practical personal habits they developed to maintain a healthy work-life balance with their loved ones.

“No, cannot. You must get a degree first.”

That was what my parents said when I told them I wanted to be a pilot.

They weren’t against the dream – they just didn’t want me to start working without a degree to fall back on.

So I made them a promise: I’d get my degree first, then apply to the airlines.

For as long as I can remember, I had built my life around flying. I graduated from polytechnic with a near‑perfect GPA in Aviation and Business, convinced I was finally ready to head for the cockpit.

But first, National Service.

I jumped at the chance to apply for the Air Force, hoping it might lead to a career in aviation. Instead, I failed the medical assessment due to a minor condition – the criteria were much stricter for the military, as compared to commercial flying.

I completed my two years of service and went on to pursue a degree in Business and Marketing at the National University of Singapore. Determined to keep my promise to dad, I took classes every semester with no breaks, finishing my honours degree in three years instead of four.

I thought I was speeding towards my dream – but the timing could not have been worse.

By the time I graduated in June 2020, the world had changed. Covid-19 hit, the aviation industry froze, and that path was no longer realistic. Peers who had joined airlines ahead of me were laid off one by one.

Clearly, I was not going to be a pilot. I also couldn’t find a job related to what I studied in university.

So, I started over.

Software engineering felt like a good next step. I had no coding background, and honestly, it felt like starting from zero. I spent weeks learning on my own, studying university textbook that I had borrowed from friends.

I applied what I learnt to build my first portfolio website, and it helped me secure an SGUnited traineeship with DBS later that same year.

From there, I joined the DBS Skill Enhancement Education & Development (SEED) programme, which led me to a full‑time job at DBS as a software engineer in 2021.

The learning curve was steep. There were times I wondered if I – a self-trained engineer – really belonged. Over time, I realised that no one really has everything figured out – you just keep learning, keep trying, and slowly get better.

Today, I work on cross-border payments systems, building backend services that support real transactions across countries. It’s a space where things have to work – not just in theory, but in real life.

As the systems grew, I started using AI tools to help with testing, debugging, and validation – the repetitive parts of the work – so I could spend more time thinking through design and solving more complex problems.

I think of AI as a useful co-pilot – it can reduce toil and execute my commands, but it doesn’t replace judgement. At the end of the day, it’s not just about the code. I ‘m still responsible for where things go. I’m still the one flying the plane.

Expanding into new markets means thinking about people, regulations, and how everything comes together in one, balanced ecosystem.

It’s been a long journey, and I’m currently pursuing a Master’s in Analytics at Georgia Tech to deepen my understanding of data and decision-making.

Looking back, things didn’t turn out the way I expected, but they turned out in a way I’m grateful for. I’m excited for what’s ahead, and for where this flight path continues to lead.

– Derek Ang, Application Developer (Remittance), DBS #HumansofDBS
