Optimizing Scam Resilience for
Vulnerable Demographics: An Analytical
Study of DBS-POSB Anti-Scam
Frameworks and Money Mule Mitigation
for Work Permit Holders in Singapore
Singapore’s hyper-connected financial ecosystem, characterized by instant payment rails like
FAST and PayNow, provides an efficient environment for both retail commerce and illicit capital
repatriation.1 The scale of this threat is substantial: in the first half of 2025 alone, Singapore
recorded over 19,600 scam cases with total customer losses reaching S$456.4 million.3 A key
component of these criminal operations is the recruitment and utilization of "money
mules"—individuals who allow criminal syndicates to use their bank accounts to transfer illicit
funds.1
Among the demographics residing in Singapore, Work Permit Holders (WPHs), including
migrant construction workers and migrant domestic workers (MDWs), are highly targeted by
money mule syndicates.6 POSB, as the primary bank for this segment, holds over a 70% market
share of all WPH bank accounts.7 The bank has developed a comprehensive anti-scam defense
system designed to address the specific vulnerabilities of these workers.7 This system combines
tailored daily transaction limits, advanced anti-malware protections, preemptive SMS warnings,
and community-led digital literacy initiatives.9
Tailored Transactional and Withdrawal Limits for Work
Permit Holders
A core element of the defense strategy is the design of the POSB Payroll Account, which is
customized for the Work Permit segment.9 While standard retail accounts feature default
transaction caps designed for higher-income users, the WPH payroll account utilizes low daily
default limits.9 This structural approach acts as an automatic buffer, limiting the potential volume
of unauthorized fund transfers or physical cash extractions if an account is compromised.2

To prevent these default limits from being instantly changed by unauthorized parties,
DBS-POSB introduced a mandatory 12-hour cooling-off period on March 7, 2026, for high-risk
digital modifications.19 This protocol delays any request to increase daily local or overseas
transfer limits, add a new transaction recipient, or update contact details like mobile numbers
and email addresses.19
If an unauthorized modification is attempted, the 12-hour delay provides an essential
intervention window.19 During this period, real-time alerts are sent to the customer's registered
contact points, allowing them to detect the activity and activate the Safety Switch to lock the
account before the changes take effect.8
Preemptive Warnings, Mobile Card Toggles, and
Temporal SMS Splits
To prevent consumers from making hasty transfers under the influence of scammers, the bank
has integrated behavioral controls into its digital messaging and notification channels.15
Scammers often use high-pressure tactics to keep victims in an impulsive state.15 To counter
this, DBS uses a "temporal split" for SMS-based transaction alerts and One-Time Passwords
(OTPs).15 Rather than delivering all transaction details and authorization codes in a single
message, the notification is divided into two parts.15 This creates a deliberate pause, allowing
the customer time to read the transaction alert details before receiving the OTP.15 This brief
delay helps move the user from an impulsive state to a more reflective one, increasing the
likelihood that they will spot potential fraud before entering the authorization code.15
The bank has also implemented strict controls for mobile wallet enrollment.19 To add a
DBS-POSB card to a digital wallet, the customer must manually activate a specific toggle within
the secure digibank mobile app.19 Once enabled, this setting automatically switches off after 10
minutes.19 This time-limited window prevents background card enrollment by remote scammers
who may have obtained card credentials but lack access to the active banking application.19
At the network level, the bank's specialized Anti-Mule Team works closely with the Singapore
Police Force’s (SPF) Anti-Scam Centre (ASC) to identify and block compromised accounts.4 A

common vulnerability involves "expired permit" accounts, where foreign workers who have
completed their contracts sell their bank accounts and Singpass details before returning home.23
This leaves "clean," verified bank accounts in the hands of scammers, making them difficult to
detect through standard initial verification checks.23
DBS uses data analytics to analyze fund flows, login device IDs, and geographic anomalies to
identify these networks.7 The Anti-Scam Team also conducts undercover tracking of suspicious
advertisements.23 For example, investigators have engaged directly with fake online
fixed-deposit advertisements to trace the destination accounts.23 Once identified as
compromised foreign worker accounts, these facilities are immediately frozen, recovering victim
funds before they can be moved overseas.4
Sideloading Protections and Mobile Application
Restrictions
As malware-enabled scams have become more prevalent, scammers have increasingly used
malicious links to prompt users to download third-party applications.11 These applications can
compromise the device's operating system, allowing scammers to capture login details and
intercept OTPs.11
To counter this threat, DBS-POSB integrated automated device-scanning features directly into
its digibank and DBS IDEAL applications.11 Upon launch, the application checks the device's
security status and will restrict access under several key conditions:
●​ Sideloaded Apps with Accessibility Permissions: Access to the banking app is
blocked if it detects applications downloaded from third-party or unauthorized sites
(sideloaded APKs) that also have Android Accessibility permissions enabled.11
Sideloading is a major security risk because these permissions can allow a malicious app
to read screen content, capture keystrokes, and execute transactions without the user's
consent.11 To restore access, the user must either uninstall the flagged application or
disable its accessibility settings.25
●​ Active Screen-Sharing or Mirroring: If the system detects active screen-sharing or
mirroring (via applications like Zoom, TeamViewer, or casting software), digibank access
is suspended immediately.11 This prevents scammers from viewing credentials or
transaction details in real time.11 If the user is not actively screen-sharing, this detection
serves as a critical warning that a remote-access Trojan may be running in the
background, prompting an immediate call to the fraud hotline.24
●​ Rooted or Jailbroken Devices: Devices with modified operating systems are blocked
from accessing the app.11 Rooting or jailbreaking bypasses the mobile OS's core security
controls, leaving the banking app vulnerable to data theft by other installed applications.11
●​ Developer Options and Virtual Environments: The application restricts access if
developer options are enabled or if the app is running within a virtual workspace.25 These
environments can be used by advanced malware to hide malicious activity from security
software.25

National Policy Frameworks and Legal Deterrents
DBS-POSB's anti-scam measures operate within Singapore's national regulatory and legal
frameworks, particularly those established by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) and
the Singapore Police Force (SPF).2
The Shared Responsibility Framework (SRF)
Implemented on December 16, 2024, the SRF establishes clear guidelines for financial loss
sharing in digital phishing scams.21 It outlines the specific security obligations of both banks and
telecommunications providers.21 Under the SRF, DBS must maintain critical baseline security
controls, such as enforcing the 12-hour cooling-off period after digital token activation, sending
real-time alerts for high-risk modifications, and providing a 24/7 reporting channel with a digital
Safety Switch.21
If the bank fails to meet any of these specified obligations during a phishing event, it must bear
the full financial loss.21 Conversely, if the bank is fully compliant, liability is assessed based on
whether other parties, such as telcos or the customer, met their respective responsibilities.21
The Facility Restriction Framework (FRF)
Introduced in phases starting October 2025, the FRF targets money mules and repeat offenders
directly.2 Under this framework, the SPF can place individuals who have facilitated scams under
strict access restrictions across financial, telecommunications, and government services.2
These restrictions are calibrated to prevent the movement of illicit funds while preserving basic
communication and survival needs 2:

Legislative Penalties and Deterrents
Singapore's legal system has introduced severe penalties for money muling to reinforce that
allowing others to use one's bank account carries serious consequences.4 Under the
Corruption, Drug Trafficking and Other Serious Crimes (Confiscation of Benefits) Act (CDSA),
individuals found guilty of assisting another to retain criminal benefits face up to 10 years in
prison, fines of up to S$500,000, or both.29
Additionally, the Computer Misuse Act (CMA) penalizes the unauthorized disclosure of Singpass
passwords or banking access codes with fines of up to S$10,000 and up to three years of
imprisonment.32
To further deter syndicate recruiters and active participants, legislative updates effective
December 30, 2025, mandate caning of at least 6 strokes (up to 24 strokes) for scam recruiters
and syndicate members.29 Scam mules who facilitate these operations by providing Singpass
credentials, bank details, or local SIM cards face discretionary caning of up to 12 strokes,
highlighting the serious nature of these offenses.29
Peer-to-Peer Educational Models and Digital Inclusion
Because technical controls cannot completely prevent scams that rely on social engineering and
victim consent, DBS-POSB also focuses on financial and digital literacy.10 These educational
efforts are designed to help WPHs recognize scam tactics and manage their money safely.6
The MiDigi Ambassador Programme
The bank runs the MiDigi Ambassador Programme to address digital banking safety and
language barriers within the migrant worker community.12 Recognizing that workers are often
hesitant to adopt digital banking due to fear of mistakes or scams, the program uses a peer-led
education model.12
●​ Dormitory and Cultural Workshops: During major cultural events like Deepavali, POSB
banking specialists host digital literacy sessions at migrant worker recreation clubs.12
●​ Ambassador Training: Select workers are trained as MiDigi Ambassadors.12 These
ambassadors then help educate their peers, creating a network of support within
dormitories to help others use the digibank app securely.12

●​ Practical Training Focus: Workshops focus on practical safety skills, including
navigating the digibank mobile app, customizing transaction alerts, utilizing card payment
controls, and identifying fake job or investment offers.10
This approach has contributed to steady digital adoption: as of late 2023, 73% of migrant
workers and foreign domestic workers actively used the DBS-POSB mobile banking app, and
digital remittance transactions grew by 16% year-on-year.12
Employment Agency Initiatives and Singpass Protection
To protect Migrant Domestic Workers (MDWs) prior to their deployment, POSB partners with
major employment agencies to deliver structured training.33 Agencies hold regular workshops to
educate incoming helpers on common scam variants.33
Crucially, agency staff assist helpers in installing the ScamShield app and setting up facial
recognition for their Singpass accounts.33 Emphasizing biometric authentication helps prevent
third-party syndicates from exploiting shared passwords to open unauthorized bank accounts.32
The Ministry of Manpower (MOM) Financial Literacy Framework
These efforts are supported by the Ministry of Manpower's mandatory Settling-In Programme
(SIP) for first-time arrivals, which covers employment rights, local laws, and safe remittance
practices.34 To help domestic helpers manage their finances and avoid debt traps—which can
make them targets for unlicensed money lenders or quick-money scams—MOM advocates a
structured five-step budgeting framework.6

Conclusion
The defense of Singapore's digital banking space against scam syndicates and money mule
exploitation requires a coordinated, multi-layered approach.2 DBS-POSB's strategy
demonstrates that technical controls—such as tailored low transaction limits, sideloading
detection, and temporal SMS alerts—are most effective when combined with strong national
policy frameworks and practical digital education.9

As scam tactics continue to evolve, maintaining these layered defenses is essential to
protecting vulnerable depositor segments and preserving trust in Singapore's financial
infrastructure.4

Corporate Profile and Strategic Context
DBS Group Holdings Ltd (headquartered in Singapore) is Southeast Asia’s largest banking and
financial services corporation by assets, operating as a dominant multinational institution
across 19 markets in Greater China, Southeast Asia, and South Asia. Recognized as one of the
"Big Three" local banks in Singapore alongside Oversea-Chinese Banking Corporation (OCBC)
and United Overseas Bank (UOB), the institution is listed on the Singapore Exchange (SGX:
D05). DBS's largest and controlling shareholder is Temasek Holdings, Singapore's sovereign
wealth fund, which maintains a stable 29% ownership stake.
The bank’s strategic orientation is historically dual-natured: it acts as a commercial engine
driving wealth and institutional expansion throughout Asia, while preserving its foundational
mission of community-centric and domestic development.1 Operating under the corporate
brand promise "Live more, Bank less," the institution merges global scale with localized
grassroots banking.
Historical Foundations and the POSB Legacy
The Development Bank of Singapore Limited (DBS) was established on 16 July 1968 by the
Government of Singapore, initiating operations on 1 September 1968 with an initial paid-up
capital of S$100 million. Formed under the direction of Finance Minister Dr. Goh Keng Swee
and led by its first president and chairman, Hon Sui Sen, the bank was designed to take over the
industrial financing responsibilities of the Economic Development Board (EDB).2 Its core
mandate was to mobilize domestic savings to support Singapore's rapid post-independence
industrialisation, providing critical capital to foundational national projects such as Jurong
Shipyard, National Iron and Steel Mills, and early urban development and tourism schemes.2 In
June 1969, the bank expanded its operations into commercial banking, establishing its first
physical retail branch in the Jurong industrial estate in 1972.2
To solidify its domestic deposit market share and establish an unassailable retail foundation,
DBS completed a historic, state-backed merger on 16 November 1998, acquiring the Post
Office Savings Bank (POSB) for S$1.6 billion. Originally established in 1877 by the British colonial
government to foster savings among lower-income groups, POSB had evolved into Singapore's
most beloved financial institution, widely referred to as the "People's Bank".

years of global banking experience across London, Tokyo, and Hong Kong, Tan spent over a
decade at DBS building its Wealth Management franchise and running both the Consumer
Banking and Institutional Banking divisions—which collectively generate approximately 90% of
the bank's total income.7
Tan’s corporate strategy centers on maintaining the digital achievements of her predecessor
while refocusing on core Asian growth regions, particularly China, India, and Indonesia.1 She
advocates for the strategic concept of an "AI-enabled bank with a heart," seeking to fuse
advanced artificial intelligence (AI) with human empathy and the bank's developmental roots.1
Her leadership has received significant global and public recognition. In late 2025, she was
ranked 6th globally in Fortune’s Most Powerful Women in Business list (the only Singaporean in
the top 10) 9 and was named Fortune’s most powerful woman in Asia.9
In May 2026, Tan cashing out a portion of her equity attracted public attention when she sold
100,000 shares on the open market at S
6 million. This
transaction trimmed her direct shareholding in the bank to 0.048%. This move coincided with
DBS stock trading at historic highs, crossing the S$60 mark in mid-May 2026 following stellar
Q1 2026 financial disclosures.
In her first full year as CEO, Tan received S

975,250, a cash bonus of S
4.9 million in deferred awards. Her
predecessor, Piyush Gupta, received S$4.2 million for his partial FY2025 tenure prior to the
March transition.10
Financial Performance and Capital Dynamics
DBS’s financial performance leading into mid-2026 demonstrates strong structural resilience.
Despite global interest rate easing and a strengthening Singapore dollar, the bank has
leveraged its non-interest fee lines to offset compression in net interest margins (NIM).
For the full year ended 31 December 2025, DBS reported a record total income of S
11.0 billion, reflecting rate headwinds and increased tax expenses
associated with the implementation of the global minimum tax framework.11 Return on equity
(ROE) remained high at 16.2%.11 Supported by active capital management, the bank's total
dividend for FY2025 rose 38% to S
0.60 per share.5
This momentum accelerated in Q1 2026 (ended 31 March 2026), with the bank posting a net
profit of S
5.95 billion, as rate-related headwinds were
successfully mitigated by a spectacular expansion in non-interest income, which climbed 10%
YoY to S$2.45 billion.
The primary engine of this growth was a record-breaking performance in the bank’s wealth
management division. Driven by regional wealth inflows and a growing affluent customer base,
Q1 2026 wealth management fees surged to a record S
1.48 billion.
Treasury customer sales also reached a historic peak of S$592 million.

Technological Transformation and the Outage
Controversy
DBS's modern operating model is built upon its "Digital to the Core" technology shift, guided by
the internal mnemonic "GANDALF".1 Seeking to operate as a technology firm rather than a
traditional bank, the organization restructured its legacy systems into a modular cloud
architecture divided into 33 platforms, each led by a business and a technology executive
under a "two-in-a-box" model.1 Leveraging its industrialized AI and machine learning platform,
known as ALAN, DBS successfully shortened its model deployment lifecycle from 18 months to
under three months.1 By the end of 2025, the bank was running more than 430 AI use cases
across over 2,000 models, generating approximately S$1 billion in economic value through
enhanced risk management, anti-money laundering (AML) controls, and hyper-personalized
consumer nudges.
Despite these digital achievements, DBS's rapid technological integration has introduced
operational stability challenges, resulting in highly visible service disruptions that have drawn
public criticism and regulatory penalties.

The bank experienced multiple high-profile digital outages between 2021 and 2025, with a
major October 2023 data center cooling failure blocking 2.5 million transactions and leading to
severe regulatory intervention by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS).3 MAS imposed a
1.8 times multiplier on the bank's risk-weighted assets for operational risk—effectively locking
up S
80 million resilience budget and modernized its technology risk governance, the S$1.6 billion
capital penalty remains in place as the bank works on multi-year systems simplification.
This digital vulnerability was highlighted again on 19 March 2026, when DBS suffered its first
major digital banking outage of 2026. Beginning around midday, the lunchtime disruption
rendered the bank’s mobile applications, online banking portals, and digital payment gateways
(including DBS PayLah! and PayNow) inaccessible to thousands of users across Singapore.
Widespread service reports on Downdetector peaked with over 2,100 complaints by 12:30 PM.
DBS issued a formal statement at 1:00 PM confirming the disruption, assuring the public that
customer funds remained safe, and outlining manual workarounds. While ATMs, physical card
payments, and over-the-counter services remained operational, the outage caused substantial
public inconvenience, particularly at food and retail outlets where cashless payments are the
norm. Numerous customers reported being unable to settle bills, leading to embarrassing
situations at hawker centers and retail shops. Services were fully restored by 1:19 PM,
representing a one-hour disruption. Following the event, MAS stated it was actively monitoring
the bank and coordinating to ensure that DBS identifies and resolves the root cause of the
incident.
Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) and
Community Integration
DBS’s corporate citizenship model is deeply integrated into its business practices, organized
around three pillars: Responsible Banking, Responsible Business Practices, and Impact Beyond
Banking.14 Globally recognized for its social initiatives, the bank was named the "World's Best
Bank for Corporate Responsibility" by Euromoney.15
Rather than treating sustainability as a secondary priority, DBS utilizes transition finance to
capture market share in Asia’s green transition while deploying social capital directly into
regional communities.14

Workplace Culture and Employee Sentiment
The execution of DBS’s digital and regional expansion is supported by a structured,
high-performance workplace culture. Through targeted digital training academies (such as
"Digify"), DBS has prioritized internal upskilling to prepare its workforce for the generative AI
era.1 In May 2026, Tan Su Shan emphasized that AI adoption allows young graduates to learn
faster, contribute earlier, and take on higher-value advisory roles from the outset.18
Comprehensive employee feedback from major platform ratings reveals a stable rating of 3.5
out of 5 stars across dimensions like culture, job security, and career progression, with pay and
benefits scoring higher at 3.7 stars. Feedback highlights distinct operational strengths and
challenges:
●​ Compensation and Benefits: Personnel consistently praise the bank for offering
competitive, above-market compensation packages, excellent performance bonuses,
and supportive policies, including flexible work arrangements and full maternity and
family wellness coverage.
●​ Learning Curve and Progression: Employees view DBS as an excellent incubator for
professional development, providing rich exposure to cutting-edge tech stacks, agile
workflows, and cross-border project management.
●​ Workplace Stress and Performance Pressures: Client-facing sales representatives
(such as Wealth Planning Managers) and tech engineers (SRE and system maintenance
teams) frequently report significant work pressure, rigid KPI targets, and long hours.
●​ Organizational Friction: Employees in offshore technology hubs sometimes report
administrative friction, micro-management, and coordination challenges with regional
management.

On one side, DBS is perceived globally as a highly secure, premier private banking and wealth
management hub. Amidst geopolitical instability in the Middle East and structural shifts in
European wealth centers, global capital flows have decisively shifted toward Singapore.
Winning the "World's Best Private Bank" award at the Euromoney Private Banking Awards 2026,
DBS's wealth division is highly valued for combining record growth with safety—pairing an AA-
credit rating with Singapore's AAA jurisdiction. For global investors and high-net-worth families,
DBS represents a stable, highly profitable institution that continues to deliver excellent capital
returns.
Locally, however, public perception is subject to recurring tension. The bank's rapid digital
scaling has outpaced its system architecture simplification, making it vulnerable to intermittent
outages like the lunchtime disruption on 19 March 2026. These events draw sharp, highly vocal
public criticism on social media, particularly when local transactions fail during daily activities
like buying meals. This operational friction keeps the bank under close regulatory scrutiny by
MAS.
Yet, the core stabilizing factor of DBS’s public perception remains its deep-rooted connection
to the community.6 By maintaining the POSB retail franchise and directing substantial capital
toward the elderly, disabled, and underprivileged through its S$1 billion foundation
commitments, DBS is not viewed simply as a corporate entity, but as a trusted national
institution.6 Moving forward, the bank's long-term success will rely on its ability to balance this
trusted, community-focused identity with the rigorous technical demands of a digital-first
global bank.
